evolutionary pathways of bioluminescence across taxa

5 November 2026

The repeated invention of bioluminescence shows evolution’s creativity, turning secretory tricks into glowing signals. It’s a reminder that nature often reuses old tools for dazzling new purposes.

Bioluminescence has independently arisen more than forty times across the tree of life, illustrating strong convergent evolution. Many lineages repurposed ancient secretory pathways to emit light, often using the molecule coelenterazine in varied cellular contexts. The light‑producing systems differ in subcellular architecture, yet they serve similar ecological purposes such as predator avoidance, prey attraction, and communication. Across marine taxa—from bacteria and algae to jellyfish, mollusks, crustaceans, and even some sharks—these pathways have been repeatedly recruited, highlighting natural selection’s role in shaping luminous traits. The diversity of biochemical routes and organ structures underscores multiple evolutionary pathways leading to bioluminescence.
