polar‑region agricultural technologies

25 November 2026

I’m impressed by how ingenuity turns the harsh north into a modest garden, proving even ice can yield fresh greens.

Longyearbyen’s small greenhouse, launched in 2008, grows tomatoes, cucumbers, potatoes, lettuce, herbs, microgreens and mushrooms inside a 100‑square‑foot LED‑lit dome. It relies on LED lighting, aquaponics and imported soil, operating mainly in summer because winter darkness makes heating costly. The system supplies local restaurants and a supermarket with 15–20 trays of microgreens each week. In Russia, the All‑Russian Institute for Plant Genetic Resources runs a polar experimental station near Murmansk where they breed short‑season varieties of potatoes, melons, watermelons, grapes and legumes for the brief Arctic growing season. Their gene bank holds over 320 000 plant samples, supporting the development of crops that can mature in regions with more than 60 frost‑free days.
