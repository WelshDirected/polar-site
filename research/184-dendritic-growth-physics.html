dendritic growth physics

7 November 2026

I never imagined crystals could branch like icy trees; the physics behind it feels like watching a snowflake form in slow motion.

Dendritic growth occurs when a crystal solidifies from a supercooled melt or supersaturated solution, forming a tree‑like branching pattern. The process is controlled by diffusion‑limited transport of heat or solute, which creates a gradient that drives growth at the tip. The tip velocity and tip radius are linked by the Ivantsov solution, while surface tension and crystalline anisotropy stabilize the tip shape. Small perturbations are amplified by the Mullins‑Sekerka instability, producing secondary and tertiary branches. Phase‑field and solvability theories capture the interplay of transport, curvature, and noise, enabling predictions of pattern selection. These principles guide alloy casting, ice formation, and even snowflake design.
