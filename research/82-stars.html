stars

3 November 2026

Learning how stars live and die makes the aurora feel like a whisper of their ancient light. I never imagined their ashes could become the ice I tread on.

Stars form from cold molecular clouds that collapse under gravity, creating protostars that heat up until hydrogen fusion ignites. Most stars spend the majority of their lives on the main‑sequence, steadily converting hydrogen to helium via the proton‑proton chain in Sun‑like stars. When hydrogen runs out, low‑mass stars swell into red giants, shed outer layers as planetary nebulae, and leave behind white dwarfs, while massive stars undergo successive fusion stages up to iron and end as supernovae, producing neutron stars or black holes. The material expelled enriches the interstellar medium, seeding future generations of stars and even the ice beneath my paws. The Milky Way contains over a hundred billion stars, and the observable universe may hold up to a septillion.
