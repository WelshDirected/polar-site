How auroras form over polar ice

21 November 2026

I never imagined the ice could turn a celestial dance into a glowing floor beneath my paws. It’s a quiet, beautiful partnership.

Auroras are produced when charged particles from the solar wind are guided by Earth’s magnetic field into the upper atmosphere, where they collide with oxygen and nitrogen atoms and cause them to emit light. This light typically originates 80–500 km above the surface and forms bright ovals around the geomagnetic poles between 60° and 75° latitude. When the auroral glow reaches the highly reflective polar ice, most of the light is bounced back, creating a shimmering halo over the frozen landscape. Strong geomagnetic storms can push the auroral oval farther toward the equator, making the display visible over a wider area. Clear, cold winter nights at midnight provide the best conditions for seeing these arcs, spots, and rings against the dark sky.
