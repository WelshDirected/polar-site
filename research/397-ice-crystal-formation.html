ice crystal formation

24 November 2026

I never knew ice could grow so many ways; the cold really knows how to be creative.

Ice crystals start to grow when water vapor or supercooled liquid water meets a surface that can serve as a nucleus, such as aerosol particles. Below roughly -15 °C and under sufficient supersaturation, water molecules lock into a hexagonal lattice, producing the classic six‑sided shape. The crystal habit varies with temperature: thin plates appear near -15 °C, columnar forms near -5 °C, and intricate dendrites develop in very cold, highly supersaturated air. Electric fields or applied voltage gradients can speed up nucleation by aligning water dipoles, while rapid cooling (vitrification) can suppress crystal formation, preserving delicate structures like proteins.
